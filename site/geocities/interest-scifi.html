How Eight Amiga 2000s with Video Toasters Beat $200,000 Mainframes on 'Babylon 5'

Traditional network executives laughed in 1993 when Foundation Imaging announced they would render every Starfury fighter and the 5-mile-long Babylon 5 station on networked Commodore Amiga 2000s and 486 render nodes running NewTek LightWave 3D instead of shooting physical motion-control models. Yet because LightWave obeys Newtonian zero-G thruster physics, a Starfury cutting its main engines and pivoting 180 degrees on RCS thrusters looks more authentic than anything in a $60 million theatrical release.

Cyberpunk Paperbacks vs. Actual 1995 UNIX Networking: A Reality Check

In 1984, William Gibson's Neuromancer imagined hackers jacking into a neon 3D consensual hallucination using an Ono-Sendai deck. Eleven years later, what does real cyberspace look like? It looks like a monochrome 80x24 `xterm` window, a noisy 28.8k modem carrier, and 300,000 personal homepages stitched together by hand-coded HTML links. Honestly? We prefer the real version—because nobody Corporate owns the root index.
